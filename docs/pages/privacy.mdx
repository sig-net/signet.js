# Privacy Model

What an application built this way hides, and what it cannot. This page summarises the [formal analysis of the ERC20 vault](https://github.com/sig-net/midnight-examples/blob/acbdf742f075cdcb2b80ae9ab35e8b9cc0103fc3/examples/erc20-vault/docs/formal-analysis/main.pdf) by Alessandra Scafuro, which defines the unlinkability and soundness a public-to-private cross chain system can achieve and analyses the [ERC20 Vault](/midnight/erc20-vault) against that definition.

The analysis covers the vault contract at release `erc20-vault-v0.3.0`. The latest release, `erc20-vault-v0.4.0`, is not formally analysed: the analysis lists what changed and expects its proof strategy to extend.

## Two kinds of unlinkability

Actions start on a private chain and take effect on a public one, so the amounts are revealed. That leaves two questions:

- **Transaction unlinkability**: can an observer tie a withdrawal to a user? This can depend on amounts and on the order of events, and cannot be a property of a protocol. If the public chain records only two deposits, one of 10 and one of 6, and later a withdrawal of 7 to a fresh account, the withdrawal came from the depositor of 10, regardless of the cryptography involved.
- **Cryptographic unlinkability**: do the protocol messages give any additional help, besides what can be inferred from the public amounts?

The analysis targets the second. It argues, in a semi-formal proof, that it is computationally infeasible to guess the shielded account behind a withdrawal, a swap, a supply or a redeem, even for an adversary who knows every deposit and every action of all honest users and takes part in the system as a malicious user.

## What a user should know

- **All amounts are public.** Withdraw, swap, supply and redeem can be called anonymously on Midnight, but they execute on the public chain, so their amounts are public even when the identity is not.
- **Amounts and timing can link actions.** Depositing 5 USD and immediately withdrawing 5 USD to a fresh Ethereum account still links the two through the amount and the timing.
- **A withdrawal is unlinkable only to a fresh Ethereum account.** Withdrawing to an account used before connects the withdrawal to that account's earlier activity. Withdrawing to the account that funded the deposit links deposit and withdrawal.
- **Keep private coins consolidated in one coin.** The number of coins collected to pay for an action leaks information about who is paying. This holds on Midnight with or without this system.
- **Deposit requests are linkable to each other.** Each user has a fixed identity commitment that every deposit uses, so all deposits by one user are linkable. Other actions are not, since each carries a one-time authentication tag.
- **The user's secret must stay secret.** Someone who learns it can link all of that user's previous activity. They can steal at most one pending request.
- **The anonymity set is the people using the same tokens.** For swap, supply and redeem it is restricted to those transacting in those particular tokens.

## What a developer should know

A fixed derivation path per user is a design choice. The vault's client picks one permanent application secret and uses it for every deposit, which favours convenience over stronger privacy: the user funds a single derived account. The cost is that all deposits to that account are linkable, even from different Ethereum accounts, and that a leaked secret deanonymises the user's past activity. A contract that derives a fresh path per deposit trades that convenience back for privacy (see [Derived Keys](/midnight/protocol-flow#derived-keys)).

## What the analysis assumes

- The Sig Network MPC is treated as a trusted signing system, and the components that emit and process chain events are trusted.
- The unlinkability results hold under the assumption that the network is anonymised.
- The proof identifies the cryptographic properties it needs from Midnight's shielded transaction and shielded computation layers and relies on them. It does not prove that those components achieve them.

The analysis describes itself as a treatment of the vault contract, not of the entire system, with some arguments left informal.
